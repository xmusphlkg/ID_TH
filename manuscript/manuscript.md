# A counterfactual framework for post-disruption recovery assessment in infectious disease surveillance in Thailand

---

## Abstract

Routine infectious disease dashboards display current incidence but not whether reported burden has rejoined its expected trajectory after disruption. We developed and retrospectively evaluated a counterfactual digital decision-support framework for Thailand's national surveillance system, analysing 43 diseases and modelling 24 with stable pre-pandemic series that accounted for 98.30% of cumulative reported cases. The framework combines disease-specific forecasting, dual recovery milestones, uncertainty propagation, seasonal displacement detection, placebo falsification, and an interface for portfolio review. Under incidence-only review, 22 diseases appeared normalized; the framework retained only 8 in routine review and reassigned 14 to cumulative review or recalibration queues. Return to Pre-pandemic baseline (RP) and Balance of Pre-pandemic cumulative deficit (BP) classifications were invariant to alternative model-selection aggregation rules, interruption-date checks, and RP threshold or persistence settings, while placebo analyses showed near-nominal mean 95% predictive-interval coverage (0.951-0.988) but 15-17 false alerts across 24 diseases under no-disruption windows. This framework supports auditable post-disruption triage and seasonal surveillance recalibration using routine digital public health data; current evidence speaks more directly to surveillance prioritization and implementation readiness than to downstream improvement in public-health outcomes.

---

## Introduction

National infectious disease surveillance systems have evolved into large-scale digital data infrastructures. However, many operational dashboards remain primarily display layers rather than decision layers, reporting current incidence or short-term deviations without translating these signals into explicit, auditable review priorities after major system disruptions.<sup>1,2</sup> From a digital medicine perspective, the key unmet need is not only to visualize surveillance data, but to operationalize them into reproducible triage logic that supports portfolio-level decision-making, specifically determining which diseases warrant routine monitoring, cumulative follow-up, or seasonal recalibration.<sup>3-5</sup>

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic exposed this limitation at scale. Non-pharmaceutical interventions, mobility restrictions, school closures, shifts in healthcare-seeking behaviour, diagnostic reprioritization, and disruptions to routine services altered both true transmission dynamics and reported surveillance incidence across a wide range of infections.<sup>6-10</sup> Despite this, most post-pandemic analyses have remained pathogen-specific, descriptive, or limited to a narrow subset of respiratory infections.<sup>3,6,7</sup> Such approaches provide limited support for portfolio-level surveillance workflows, where public-health teams must systematically compare multiple diseases, prioritise follow-up, and justify escalation decisions even when monthly incidence appears to have returned to expected levels.

A second challenge is conceptual. "Recovery" is often treated as a single endpoint and is sometimes interpreted through mechanistic constructs such as immunity debt.<sup>11</sup> However, routine surveillance data are not well suited to establishing true infection burden recovery or underlying biological mechanisms. Instead, they are better positioned to define operational surveillance states. For decision-support purposes, at least two distinct recovery processes are relevant: (i) realignment of reported monthly incidence with expected levels (reported-incidence normalization), and (ii) closure of the cumulative observed-minus-expected deficit accrued during disruption (cumulative deficit closure). These processes are not necessarily synchronous and may coexist with persistent seasonal displacement, which can shift the timing of expected peaks and require recalibration of alert thresholds.

Existing analytical tools address adjacent but different surveillance tasks. Classical aberration-detection methods such as Farrington, EARS, and CUSUM are primarily designed for near-real-time exceedance detection rather than for judging whether multi-year disruption deficits have closed or seasonal timing has realigned.<sup>1,23-25</sup> BSTS and related CausalImpact-style counterfactual designs can estimate intervention-related deviations,<sup>15,26</sup> whereas Bayesian nowcasting and reporting-delay models focus on incomplete recent counts and timeliness correction.<sup>27,28</sup> These strands are therefore complementary to, rather than replacements for, a portfolio-level recovery review layer.

In this study, we develop and retrospectively evaluate a counterfactual framework for post-disruption recovery assessment in infectious disease surveillance using routine national data. Drawing on Thailand's notifiable disease surveillance system, we analysed 43 infectious diseases from 2008 to 2025 and modelled 24 diseases with stable pre-pandemic monthly time series. We examine whether an uncertainty-aware, dual-metric framework can transform retrospective surveillance data into an auditable disease-review queue by distinguishing monthly normalization from cumulative reconciliation, quantifying classification uncertainty, identifying diseases requiring cumulative follow-up or seasonal recalibration, and enabling reproducible prioritization across a national surveillance portfolio.

## Results

### High-burden diseases required heterogeneous counterfactual baselines

A subset of 24 diseases with stable pre-pandemic time series accounted for 98.30% of cumulative reported cases and formed the core modelling set (Supplementary Table S1; Supplementary Fig. S0; Supplementary Tables S2-S3). This counterfactual layer therefore covered the high-burden core of the national surveillance portfolio while leaving sparse or short-duration diseases in descriptive monitoring rather than removing them from review. The 19 diseases retained descriptively but not modelled counterfactually were concentrated in sparse, short, non-seasonal, or residual series rather than a single transmission group, indicating that model exclusion reflected time-series suitability rather than de-prioritization of specific disease classes (Supplementary Table S3). By design, this screening also reduced the influence of low-denominator instability on forecast ranking by leaving structurally sparse series outside the counterfactual selection step. Disease-specific model selection identified a heterogeneous set of optimal forecasting families rather than a single dominant specification: weighted hybrid ensemble and exponential smoothing were each selected for 6 diseases, the trigonometric seasonality, Box-Cox transformation, autoregressive moving-average errors, trend, and seasonal components (TBATS) family for 5, Bayesian structural time series for 3, autoregressive integrated moving average with Fourier terms for 3, and seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average for 1 (Fig. 2A). The median within-disease separation between the best- and worst-performing candidate families was 14.30 percentage points on the symmetric mean absolute percentage error (sMAPE) scale, supporting disease-specific rather than one-size-fits-all baselines, which is critical for portfolio-level surveillance where heterogeneous dynamics must be evaluated within a unified decision framework (Supplementary Figures S87-S110). Paired comparisons against uniform ETS and SARIMA baselines further favored adaptive model selection for 17 and 21 of the 24 diseases, respectively (Fig. 2B,C).

These baseline choices were stable to alternative aggregation rules, with the primary selected family recovered for most diseases under rank-based, sMAPE-only, and horizon-weighted selection variants (Supplementary Table S11). The upstream data-processing pipeline also showed high agreement with official monthly totals during overlap years from 2020 to 2023 (Pearson r = 0.998; median absolute error 3 cases; median absolute percentage error 3.90%), supporting the validity of the reconstructed monthly series used for downstream modelling (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7; Supplementary Figures S1-S86). Forecast uncertainty was then propagated from 5,000 simulated trajectories per disease into all recovery summaries.

### Surveillance-defined recovery was frequently decoupled across monthly and cumulative dimensions

Recovery was frequently decoupled between monthly normalization and cumulative reconciliation. Using the deterministic counterfactual median as the primary summary, the Return to Pre-pandemic baseline (RP) and Balance of Pre-pandemic cumulative deficit (BP) algorithm assigned the 24 modelled diseases to four surveillance-defined phenotypes (Fig. 3): 14 achieved both RP and BP within follow-up, 8 achieved RP without BP, 1 remained suppressed without RP, and malaria showed no sustained cumulative deficit. RP and BP were operational milestones rather than biological endpoints, but they identified substantively different surveillance states. Monthly normalization often arrived well before cumulative closure: hand, foot, and mouth disease reached RP 9 months after January 2020 but did not achieve BP until month 33, and chickenpox, influenza, mumps, dengue fever, hepatitis A virus (HAV), shigellosis, scarlet fever, and chancroid all met RP criteria while remaining cumulatively unresolved at the end of follow-up.

### Framework-based review changed surveillance priorities beyond incidence-only normalization

Incidence-only surveillance would have substantially misclassified post-disruption recovery. By the end of follow-up, 22 of the 24 modelled diseases appeared operationally normalized on monthly incidence alone, but the framework retained only 8 of these 22 in the low-priority routine-review queue and reassigned the remaining 14 to cumulative review, recalibration, or manual-review queues with explicit reasons (Supplementary Table S12). This amounted to decision discordance for 58.33% of the full 24-disease portfolio and for 63.64% of diseases that would otherwise have been treated as normalized. The under-triage signal was concentrated in the 8 RP-without-BP diseases, which appeared recovered on monthly incidence but still carried unresolved cumulative deficits, and in 6 balanced diseases that still required seasonal recalibration despite achieving BP. Table 1 summarizes the resulting disease-level review queue.

This differentiation persisted when the framework was evaluated at earlier decision freeze points. Using only information available by December 2023, the framework queue captured all 17 diseases that still required continued review by December 2024, whereas the incidence-only comparator captured 6, corresponding to overall decision accuracies of 0.958 and 0.542, respectively. At the June 2024 freeze point, the framework captured 13 of 15 diseases that still required continued review by December 2025, compared with 4 of 15 under incidence-only review, with corresponding accuracies of 0.750 and 0.542 (Supplementary Table S18; Supplementary Fig. S127). These results indicate improved prioritization of follow-up within the same surveillance framework.

### Uncertainty and sensitivity analyses preserved the main recovery patterns

The deterministic RP/BP labels were informative, but their stability varied across diseases (Supplementary Table S9). HAV and influenza showed stable RP-without-BP profiles, whereas hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD), hepatitis B virus (HBV), genital herpes, *Streptococcus suis* (*S. suis*), scrub typhus, melioidosis, and typhoid showed consistently strong support for cumulative closure. Ambiguity was concentrated in a smaller subset, particularly syphilis, dengue fever, shigellosis, amebiasis, and scarlet fever, and 9 diseases had primary-status probability below 0.80 and were flagged as uncertainty-sensitive in the review layer. Uncertainty therefore qualified the strength of evidence for each phenotype without removing the central distinction between monthly and cumulative recovery.

The main classifications were also robust to alternative operational specifications. RP/BP assignments were unchanged when the monthly recovery threshold was varied across 90%, 95%, and 100%, when persistence requirements were varied from 2 to 4 months, and when the analytical interruption date was shifted from January 2020 to March or April 2020 (Supplementary Tables S5 and S10). Across all nine threshold-persistence configurations, the portfolio phenotype distribution remained identical at 14 balanced, 8 RP-only, 1 suppressed, and 1 no-deficit disease. Alternative endpoint analyses supported the same interpretation but showed that not all endpoints were equally useful for decision support: a sustained observed-to-expected ratio of at least 1.0 reproduced the same achieved-versus-not-achieved distinction as the primary RP rule across all 23 diseases that entered a cumulative deficit, whereas predictive-interval return was less discriminating and half-deficit closure behaved as an intermediate milestone (Supplementary Table S13).

Calibration and falsification results qualified this robustness. Across the three pre-pandemic placebo interruption windows, portfolio-mean empirical coverage for the nominal 95% predictive intervals ranged from 0.951 to 0.988 and for the nominal 80% intervals from 0.875 to 0.911 (Supplementary Table S19; Supplementary Fig. S128). Yet these same placebo windows still generated 15-17 false alerts and 3-13 false-suppressed classifications among the 24 diseases, indicating that near-nominal interval coverage did not eliminate spurious RP/BP states under short no-disruption windows. False alerts were observed in all four transmission groups, while group-specific mean 95% coverage remained between 0.949 and 0.991.

### External contextual triangulation supported the timing of portfolio disruption

Portfolio suppression aligned with the period of greatest external disruption. During the restriction-intensive period of 2020-2021, the mean observed-to-expected portfolio ratio across the 24 modelled diseases was 0.41, rising to 0.53 in the 2022 transition period and 1.18 in the January 2023 to June 2024 period for which World Health Organization (WHO) case data were available (Supplementary Table S14). Across months with available external data, the monthly portfolio ratio was negatively correlated with national stringency and related response indicators, supporting the interpretation that the deepest  suppression coincided with the most restrictive pandemic conditions (Supplementary Table S15). Peak stringency occurred in April 2020, the WHO COVID-19 case peak in August 2021, the first sustained low-restriction month in October 2022, and the first sustained portfolio-level normalization month in July 2023. These contextual signals were used only to interpret timing and to motivate interruption-date sensitivity analyses, not as predictive covariates.

### Seasonal displacement identified recalibration needs beyond cumulative recovery

Recovery classification alone was insufficient to determine operational surveillance readiness, because several diseases remained seasonally displaced after apparent recovery (Fig. 4). At the prespecified >=2-month cutoff, 9 of the 24 modelled diseases were flagged as seasonally shifted in relation to the pre-pandemic or post-2023 counterfactual reference profiles. Observed-to-expected ratios were below 1 for most diseases during 2020-2022 and increased from 2023 onward across all transmission categories (Fig. 4A-D). Using the weighted circular center-of-mass metric, the largest phase shifts were observed for mumps, HAV, hepatitis C virus (HCV), and condyloma acuminatum, with intermediate shifts for rubella, HBV, and genital herpes, whereas dengue fever, malaria, and *S. suis* infection showed little or no shift on this metric (Fig. 4H, J, U, X-Z, AB). Seasonal amplitude changed independently of phase timing: it was compressed for mumps, HAV, HCV, and condyloma acuminatum, but remained stable or increased for several vector-borne infections, including dengue fever, malaria, and scrub typhus.

Joint interpretation of RP/BP phenotype and seasonal displacement revealed four operational patterns that would have been missed by any single metric alone (Supplementary Table S12). Seasonally shifted but cumulatively balanced diseases, including melioidosis, HBV, condyloma acuminatum, genital herpes, HCV, and syphilis, required recalibration despite achieving BP. Mumps and HAV combined unresolved cumulative deficit with marked phase shift and therefore represented the clearest recalibrate-and-monitor targets. Chickenpox, influenza, scarlet fever, dengue fever, shigellosis, and chancroid instead fit a cumulative-review queue because their main residual issue was unresolved cumulative deficit rather than calendar displacement, whereas rubella remained a distinct manual-review case. These findings show that recovery classification alone is insufficient for operational surveillance and that seasonal displacement must be integrated into the same decision layer to align alerts and interventions with post-disruption timing.

## Discussion

The main contribution of this study is best understood as a digital public-health review-support layer rather than as proof of downstream decision improvement. Across Thailand's 24 modelled diseases, monthly normalization, cumulative reconciliation, and seasonal realignment were frequently decoupled, showing that post-disruption recovery is multidimensional and cannot be inferred from current incidence alone. By converting these dimensions into explicit review states, the framework provides an auditable basis for prioritizing follow-up across a national surveillance portfolio.

This multidimensionality is operationally important because apparently normalized incidence can coexist with unresolved cumulative deficit or shifted seasonal timing. Under those conditions, a conventional dashboard may signal reassurance too early even though the surveillance system still faces unresolved burden or misaligned alert calendars. We therefore interpret monthly normalization and cumulative balance as surveillance-defined milestones rather than biological constants,<sup>11</sup> and we view seasonal displacement as part of the same review problem rather than as a separate descriptive feature. The contextual indicators were consistent with this reading: portfolio suppression tracked the period of greatest pandemic restriction, whereas portfolio-level normalization occurred later, supporting the interpretation that surveillance pressure and reporting disruption contributed to the observed patterns.

These distinctions mattered for review prioritization. More than half of the portfolio received a different review assignment under the framework than under incidence-only normalization, indicating that the framework did not simply reproduce what a standard dashboard would already show. Freeze-point analyses at December 2023 and June 2024 suggested that this advantage persisted when review decisions were evaluated using only information available at the time. The implication is not that downstream public-health outcomes were proven to improve, but that post-disruption surveillance review can be formalized into explicit and inspectable rules rather than left to implicit judgment from incidence curves alone. Interactive implementations were developed to operationalize this workflow for portfolio review and disease-level drill-down.

This positioning also clarifies why we did not treat the study as a head-to-head comparison with all surveillance algorithms. Classical aberration detectors such as Farrington, EARS, and CUSUM are optimized to flag short-term departures from expected counts,<sup>23-25</sup> whereas our primary target was a medium-horizon review problem: whether monthly incidence had normalized, whether the cumulative deficit had been repaid, and whether seasonal timing had shifted after a known system-wide shock. Likewise, BSTS/CausalImpact-style counterfactual designs and hierarchical nowcasting models with explicit reporting-delay components address related but different tasks, including intervention attribution and correction of incomplete recent reporting.<sup>15,26-28</sup> We therefore regard these methods as complementary benchmarks for future work rather than omitted substitutes for the present framework.

The uncertainty and endpoint analyses further clarify how the framework should be used. Disease-level labels were not equally stable, and the probability summaries help distinguish high-confidence review states from borderline cases that warrant closer interpretation. Likewise, alternative endpoint analyses showed that the main conclusion was not driven by a single lenient threshold, but also that not every plausible endpoint was equally aligned with review decisions. In practice, the framework is strongest when deterministic classifications, uncertainty summaries, and endpoint sensitivity are interpreted together rather than when any single rule is treated as definitive.

The falsification results sharpen this interpretation. Portfolio-level placebo windows produced near-nominal mean 95% interval coverage, yet operational false alerts remained common. This pattern suggests that misclassification risk in this setting arises not only from interval miscalibration but also from the instability of short no-disruption windows when deterministic queue rules are applied disease by disease. For practice, that argues for using RP/BP labels together with status probabilities, seasonal flags, and manual review of low-signal series rather than treating any single alert as self-validating.

Several limitations define the scope of the evidence. First, routine surveillance counts reflect healthcare seeking, testing, and reporting as well as transmission, so this framework addresses recovery of reported surveillance burden rather than true infection burden.<sup>3,6-10</sup> Second, there was no external gold standard for correct review assignments; the freeze-point analyses evaluated consistency within the same rule system rather than validity against independent decision outcomes. Third, the forecasting layer used transformation-based general time-series families rather than explicit count-data or reporting-delay models. Upstream exclusion of sparse, weak-signal, or residual series reduced this risk, but some low-incidence diseases may still be vulnerable to sMAPE instability, residual overdispersion, and short-window false alerts. Fourth, we added empirical 80% and 95% coverage checks and placebo falsification, but horizon-specific calibration diagnostics such as probability integral transform histograms were not part of the current analysis. Fifth, monthly reconstruction may still affect fine seasonal timing, the >=2-month seasonal-shift cutoff was a high-signal monthly-resolution rule rather than a formal phase-uncertainty estimate, and the interface evaluation remained heuristic rather than prospective or user-centred. The current evidence therefore supports implementation readiness more directly than real-world impact.

The framework is likely most transferable to surveillance settings with multi-year monthly series, stable case definitions, and documented reporting continuity, but it should not be treated as a universal template. Local use would require recalibration of thresholds, queue rules, and interpretive context, and the supplementary pertussis case study should be read as evidence of workflow portability rather than external validation of the Thailand rule set. Future extensions could incorporate mobility or PHSM covariates, province-level hierarchical borrowing, or explicit delay models where sufficiently complete covariate histories exist, helping distinguish reporting perturbation from transmission change. Operationally, because models are fit offline and the dashboard serves cached outputs, the framework is most naturally implemented as a scheduled batch review process aligned to monthly or quarterly surveillance refreshes; prospective deployment should test whether probability flags, seasonal-shift alerts, and queue summaries improve time-to-review or time-to-action. Taken together, these findings argue that post-disruption surveillance recovery should be assessed as a multidimensional review problem, and that auditable review states provide a more defensible basis for surveillance prioritization than incidence normalization alone.

## Methods

### Study design, data sources, and disease selection

We conducted a retrospective development and evaluation study of a digital public-health review-support framework using publicly available aggregated surveillance data for nationally notifiable infectious diseases in Thailand. The observational-data component was reported with reference to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidance.<sup>12</sup> Monthly national case counts for 2008-2024 were assembled from the Bureau of Epidemiology surveillance portal (https://doe1.moph.go.th/surdata/index.php), and weekly surveillance data for 2020-2025 were obtained from the Department of Disease Control dashboard (https://dvis3.ddc.moph.go.th/). The source registry comprised 72 monitored disease series. Annual population denominators were taken from the United Nations World Population Prospects 2024 mid-year estimates.<sup>13</sup> Surveillance data were aggregated by disease and time; sex assigned at birth, gender identity, race, and ethnicity were not consistently available and were therefore not analysed.

Disease screening followed a two-stage protocol recorded in the analytical metadata and summarized in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S0. Stage 1 retained diseases for descriptive portfolio analysis after excluding overlapping categories, non-infectious conditions, ill-defined or residual categories, zero-incidence series, incompletely reported recent series, and a series with a surveillance-definition shift (Supplementary Table S2). The 29 diseases excluded before descriptive analysis comprised 11 overlapping categories, 6 conditions outside the transmissible-disease focus, 5 ill-defined or residual series, 3 zero-incidence series, 3 incompletely reported recent series, and 1 series with a surveillance-definition shift. Stage 2 retained diseases for counterfactual modelling when they met four prespecified operational criteria: a continuous 144-month pre-pandemic monthly series from January 2008 to December 2019, a stable surveillance definition, sufficient pre-pandemic signal to support 72-month extrapolation, and absence of dominance by structural zeros. Nineteen diseases remained in descriptive monitoring only, primarily because pre-pandemic counts were too sparse for stable long-horizon forecasting (12 diseases), because the historical signal was weakly seasonal or non-seasonal (3 diseases), because duration was insufficient (2 diseases), or because residual definitions limited interpretability (2 diseases). Diseases that failed the forecasting screen therefore remained visible in the portfolio rather than being discarded, and disease-level reason labels were preserved in Supplementary Table S3 rather than being collapsed into a single exclusion category. To extend incomplete official monthly follow-up through 2025, weekly surveillance totals were temporally disaggregated to monthly counts and validated against overlap-period official monthly series; the full reconstruction protocol and validation results are reported in the Supplementary Methods, Supplementary Tables S6-S7, and Supplementary Figures S1-S86.

### Counterfactual model selection and forecasting

For each modelled disease, counterfactual trajectories from January 2020 to December 2025 were estimated from the pre-pandemic baseline spanning January 2008 to December 2019. January 2020 was chosen a priori as the common portfolio interruption date to maximize cross-disease comparability; March 2020 and April 2020 were prespecified alternative interruption dates for sensitivity analyses. Counts were modelled on the square-root scale after adding 0.01, a negligible constant used only to retain zero-count months under transformation. This common positive-support transformation allowed diverse forecasting families to be compared on the same scale; diseases dominated by structural zeros or weak signal were excluded upstream rather than forced into counterfactual extrapolation. Model fitting was performed on transformed monthly counts, whereas forecasts, simulated trajectories, thresholds, and recovery rules were interpreted on the original count scale after back-transformation.

We compared seven candidate model families spanning autoregressive neural networks, information-criterion-selected exponential-smoothing state-space models, information-criterion-selected seasonal autoregressive integrated moving-average models, trigonometric state-space models for complex seasonality with a fixed 12-month cycle, a hybrid ensemble combining autoregressive, exponential-smoothing, neural-network, and complex-seasonality components with cross-validated root mean squared error weighting, Bayesian structural time-series models with local linear trend and seasonal states, and autoregressive models augmented with Fourier harmonic terms. For the harmonic-regression specification, the number of Fourier terms was selected by minimum corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc) over candidate values of $K$. Three rolling pre-pandemic hold-out schemes were used for model selection: training through December 2018 with validation on 2019, training through December 2017 with validation on 2018-2019, and training through December 2016 with validation on 2017-2019. Forecast accuracy was assessed on the back-transformed scale using symmetric mean absolute percentage error (sMAPE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute scaled error (MASE).<sup>16</sup>

For each disease and hold-out split, each accuracy metric was z-standardized across the seven candidate models, multiplied by $-1$ so that larger values indicated better performance, and summed with equal weight to define a split-specific composite score. Composite scores were then summed across the three hold-out schemes, and the highest-scoring model was selected as the primary disease-specific counterfactual specification. This aggregation rule was specified to balance scale-dependent and scale-free error measures within each disease rather than to privilege any single metric or horizon. Alternative aggregation rules were assessed separately in robustness analyses.

After model selection, the winning specification for each disease was refit to the full January 2008-December 2019 baseline and forecast forward to December 2025. Forecast uncertainty in the primary analysis was summarized from 5,000 simulated trajectories per disease, with 1,000 iterations for the Bayesian structural models. Residual-bootstrap forward simulation was used for the autoregressive neural-network, seasonal autoregressive integrated moving-average, trigonometric state-space, and Fourier-augmented autoregressive families; transformed-scale one-step residual bootstraps around the deterministic forecast were used for the exponential-smoothing family; historical-residual resampling around the mean forecast was used for the hybrid ensemble; and posterior predictive draws after burn-in were used for the Bayesian structural family. Monthly medians and 80% and 95% predictive intervals were obtained from the empirical quantiles of these simulated trajectories. Empirical interval calibration was then assessed in pre-pandemic placebo interruption windows by comparing observed counts with the nominal 80% and 95% predictive intervals over 12-, 24-, and 36-month placebo follow-up periods.

### RP/BP definitions, uncertainty propagation, and sensitivity analyses

We compared observed monthly counts after January 2020 with disease-specific counterfactual medians. For month $t$, observed cases were denoted by $O_t$ and the counterfactual median by $E_t$. Monthly deviation and cumulative deviation were defined as $D_t = O_t - E_t$ and $C_t = \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} D_{\tau}$, respectively. Disruption onset was defined as the first month for which $C_t < 0$, and the trough was the month at which $C_t$ reached its minimum.

Recovery phenotypes were mapped using Return to Pre-pandemic baseline (RP) and Balance of Pre-pandemic cumulative deficit (BP). RP was defined as the first month $t$ for which observed monthly counts satisfied $O_t \ge 0.95E_t$ for three consecutive months and the cumulative deviation was non-decreasing month-to-month over the same window. This additional cumulative condition was included to avoid declaring recovery in windows where monthly incidence temporarily met threshold while the cumulative deficit was still worsening. BP was defined as the first month after the trough at which $C_t \ge 0$. We treated the 95% threshold and 3-month persistence rule as prespecified operational rules for monthly surveillance review rather than as biological endpoints.

To propagate uncertainty into recovery classification, we applied the RP/BP algorithm to each of the 5,000 simulated counterfactual trajectories, yielding disease-specific probabilities of achieving RP and BP, empirical 95% intervals for RP/BP timing, and the probability of retaining the deterministic median-based phenotype. Sensitivity analyses repeated the classification under alternative interruption dates (January, March, and April 2020), alternative RP thresholds (90%, 95%, and 100%), and alternative persistence requirements (2-4 months). Additional endpoint analyses examined predictive-interval return, sustained observed-to-expected ratio of at least 1.0, and half-deficit closure. Suppression and rebound descriptors used for supplementary typology analyses were not part of the primary RP/BP decision rule.

### Seasonal displacement and retrospective decision-point assessment

Seasonal shape, amplitude, and timing were compared across three periods: pre-pandemic observed data through 2019, observed data from 2023 onward after relaxation of the most restrictive public-health and social measures (PHSM) phase, and the corresponding counterfactual predictions from 2023 onward. The 2022 calendar year was treated as a transition period and excluded from seasonal-shift estimation to reduce mixed-regime contamination. Seasonal timing was summarized with a weighted circular center-of-mass metric in which calendar months were mapped onto a 12-month circle and weighted by mean monthly counts; resulting shifts were corrected to the minimal signed displacement on the annual cycle.

For operational classification, substantial seasonal displacement was flagged a priori when the absolute center-of-mass shift relative to either the pre-pandemic observed profile or the post-2023 counterfactual profile was at least 2 months. This threshold was used as a pragmatic monthly-resolution rule: shifts smaller than about one surveillance month in either direction were unlikely to alter review timing, whereas shifts of 2 months or more could plausibly move peak-oriented preparedness windows to a different part of the annual review calendar. We therefore treated the cutoff as a high-signal recalibration flag rather than as a formal estimator of seasonal phase uncertainty. The seasonal module was applied only to the screened modelling subset, and shift flags were interpreted jointly with amplitude profiles and RP/BP phenotype rather than as standalone evidence. We then compared an incidence-only queue, in which diseases were considered operationally normalized once RP was achieved, against a framework-based queue that combined RP/BP phenotype with seasonal displacement into six review categories: low-priority routine review, cumulative review needed, recalibrate and monitor, balanced but recalibrate seasonality, high-priority manual review, and no-deficit monitoring.

To assess whether this queue structure retained value at realistic decision points, we performed retrospective freeze-point emulation at December 2023 and June 2024 using only information available up to each freeze date and then re-evaluated diseases at the end of the subsequent follow-up window under the same rule set. System-level falsification analyses repeated the workflow using placebo interruption windows beginning in January 2017, January 2018, and January 2019. Any placebo classification other than `No deficit` was counted as a false alert, and placebo classifications of `Suppressed` were also tallied separately as false-suppressed. For descriptive contextual triangulation, national Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker indicators and WHO COVID-19 case counts were aggregated to calendar months through December 2022 and used only to contextualize disruption timing rather than as predictive covariates.

### Supplementary implementation details and exploratory analyses

Interactive Shiny applications were developed to present precomputed portfolio queues, counterfactual trajectories, uncertainty summaries, and seasonal outputs for deployment-oriented review. These interfaces load bundled cached outputs rather than refitting models at runtime, and the upstream analytical scripts use multicore execution where available to support portfolio-scale reruns. The interfaces were implementation aids rather than part of the primary inferential protocol, and formal end-to-end runtime benchmarking was not part of the present evaluation. A supplementary author-side heuristic coverage check examined six predefined review tasks using a 4-point rubric for discoverability, interpretability, and auditability.

Additional exploratory analyses were reported only in the supplementary materials and did not define the main framework outputs. These included seasonal-trend decomposition using Loess (STL), segmented log-linear regression, Pearson-correlation overlays for suppression-rebound summaries, k-means clustering of standardized suppression and rebound features, and a supplementary transportability exercise applying the same model-selection and RP/BP workflow to six external pertussis surveillance series from Australia, China, Japan, New Zealand, Sweden, and the United States.<sup>20,21,22</sup> All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.2.

### Ethics approval

This study used publicly available, de-identified, aggregated surveillance data and did not involve individual participant contact or intervention. Ethical approval and individual informed consent were therefore not required under institutional policy for secondary analysis of anonymized public surveillance data.

## Figure Legends

**Figure 1. Two-panel overview of the counterfactual digital decision-support pipeline.** Operational overview of the review-facing workflow. The pipeline comprises six layers: data ingestion from Thailand's national infectious disease surveillance system; automated data processing with weekly-to-monthly temporal disaggregation and overlap-year validation; disease-specific model selection across seven forecasting families using rolling hold-out validation and composite accuracy scoring; generation of counterfactual trajectories, predictive intervals, and simulated forecast paths; analytical modules for Return to Pre-pandemic baseline (RP) and Balance of Pre-pandemic cumulative deficit (BP) classification, uncertainty-aware phenotype probabilities, alternative endpoint checks, seasonal displacement detection, and contextual triangulation; and a web-based review layer for operational surveillance prioritization.

**Figure 2. Disease-specific model selection performance.** (A) Heatmap of composite z-standardized performance indices, calculated from symmetric mean absolute percentage error (sMAPE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute scaled error (MASE) across three rolling hold-out schemes for each model family and disease. Warmer colours indicate better overall performance, and asterisks mark the selected best model for each disease. Diseases are grouped by transmission category. (B) Per-disease comparison of mean sMAPE for the selected disease-specific model, a uniform exponential smoothing (ETS) baseline, and a uniform seasonal autoregressive integrated moving-average (SARIMA) baseline. Lower sMAPE indicates better forecast accuracy. (C) Distribution of percentage-point sMAPE improvement achieved by the selected disease-specific model relative to the uniform ETS and SARIMA baselines; positive values indicate better performance of adaptive model selection.

**Figure 3. Dual-metric surveillance-defined recovery trajectories across 24 infectious diseases.** (A-X) Disease-specific panels showing monthly observed incidence (red) and the counterfactual median forecast (teal) from January 2019 to December 2025. Green and red fills indicate months in which observed incidence was below or above the counterfactual median, respectively. Blue and gold bands above each panel denote the interval from disruption-defined deficit onset to Return to Pre-pandemic baseline (RP) and Balance of Pre-pandemic cumulative deficit (BP), respectively; dashed vertical lines mark the corresponding dates, and labels report the duration in months or indicate when RP/BP was not achieved by the end of follow-up. Diseases are ordered by transmission category. Disease-level uncertainty summaries are provided in Supplementary Table S9.

**Figure 4. Seasonal displacement after coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) disruption.** (A-D) Heatmaps of monthly observed-to-expected incidence ratios for respiratory, vector-borne and zoonotic, gastrointestinal, and sexually transmitted infections. Values below 1 indicate suppression and values above 1 indicate exceedance. (E-AB) Seasonal profiles comparing pre-pandemic observed data, observed data after relaxation of the most restrictive public-health and social measures (PHSM) phase, and the corresponding post-PHSM counterfactual patterns. Triangles denote circular center-of-mass timing estimates and circles denote empirical peak months. Grey shading denotes the rainy season in Thailand. Joint interpretation of Return to Pre-pandemic baseline (RP) and Balance of Pre-pandemic cumulative deficit (BP) phenotype and seasonal displacement for operational prioritization is provided in Supplementary Table S12.

**Figure 5. Suppression extent and rebound patterns following disruption during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) period.** (A) Absolute suppression for the 23 diseases exhibiting a measurable post-January 2020 deficit. (B) Rebound intensity and relative suppression against rebound intensity. (C) Association between Return to Pre-pandemic baseline (RP) timing and rebound intensity across diseases. Pearson correlation is shown (two-sided P value). Diseases not meeting recovery criteria by the end of follow-up were right-censored in December 2025 for duration computation. (D) Cluster analysis of diseases based on relative suppression and rebound intensity. Clusters were defined using k-means ($k = 3$). This partition was selected based on the Elbow method, identifying a 38% reduction in within-cluster variance compared to $k = 2$.
