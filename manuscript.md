# A counterfactual framework for post-disruption recovery assessment in infectious disease surveillance in Thailand

---

## Abstract

Routine infectious disease dashboards display current incidence but not whether reported burden has rejoined its expected trajectory after disruption or which diseases still warrant review. We retrospectively evaluated a counterfactual digital decision-support workflow for Thailand's national surveillance system, analysing 43 diseases and modelling 24 stable pre-pandemic series that accounted for 98.30% of cumulative reported cases. The framework separated monthly normalization, cumulative deficit closure, and seasonal realignment to generate auditable review queues from routine surveillance data. By the end of follow-up, 22 diseases would have appeared normalized under incidence-only review, but only 8 remained in low-priority routine review; 14 were reassigned to cumulative follow-up, recalibration, or manual review. However, placebo windows showed sub-nominal empirical coverage (80%: 0.591-0.634; 95%: 0.747-0.802) and 15-17 false alerts per 24-disease window, reduced to 10-13 under a tempered rule, indicating that queue labels require uncertainty-aware human adjudication rather than automatic action. This framework should therefore be interpreted as an auditable, reproducible review-support prototype for surveillance prioritization and alert-calendar recalibration. Current evidence supports reproducible prioritization logic rather than externally validated operational superiority, downstream effectiveness, or autonomous deployment.

---

## Introduction

National infectious disease surveillance systems have evolved into large-scale digital data infrastructures. However, many operational dashboards remain primarily display layers rather than decision layers, reporting current incidence or short-term deviations without translating these signals into explicit, auditable review priorities after major system disruptions.<sup>1,2</sup> From a digital medicine perspective, the key unmet need is not only to visualize surveillance data, but to operationalize them into reproducible triage logic that supports portfolio-level decision-making, specifically determining which diseases warrant routine monitoring, cumulative follow-up, or seasonal recalibration.<sup>3-5</sup>

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic exposed this limitation at scale. Non-pharmaceutical interventions, mobility restrictions, school closures, shifts in healthcare-seeking behaviour, diagnostic reprioritization, and disruptions to routine services altered both true transmission dynamics and reported surveillance incidence across a wide range of infections.<sup>6-10</sup> Despite this, most post-pandemic analyses have remained pathogen-specific, descriptive, or limited to a narrow subset of respiratory infections.<sup>3,6,7</sup> Such approaches provide limited support for portfolio-level surveillance workflows, where public-health teams must systematically compare multiple diseases, prioritise follow-up, and justify escalation decisions even when monthly incidence appears to have returned to expected levels.

A second challenge is conceptual. "Recovery" is often treated as a single endpoint and is sometimes interpreted through mechanistic constructs such as immunity debt.<sup>11</sup> However, routine surveillance data are not well suited to establishing true infection burden recovery or underlying biological mechanisms. Instead, they are better positioned to define operational surveillance states. Incidence-only normalization can therefore be epidemiologically misleading after a major disruption: monthly reported counts may return to their expected range even while a large disruption-era shortfall remains unreconciled. This can occur when prolonged suppression leaves an enlarged susceptible pool, so subsequent rebound restores contemporaneous incidence before the accumulated deficit has been repaid, and also when care seeking, test availability, or diagnostic prioritization change the relationship between true transmission and reported burden.<sup>6-11</sup> For decision-support purposes, at least two distinct recovery processes are therefore relevant: (i) realignment of reported monthly incidence with expected levels (reported-incidence normalization), and (ii) closure of the cumulative observed-minus-expected deficit accrued during disruption (cumulative deficit closure). These processes are not necessarily synchronous and may coexist with persistent seasonal displacement, which can shift the timing of expected peaks and require recalibration of alert thresholds.

Existing analytical tools address adjacent but different surveillance tasks. Classical aberration-detection methods such as Farrington, EARS, and CUSUM are primarily designed for near-real-time exceedance detection rather than for judging whether multi-year disruption deficits have closed or seasonal timing has realigned.<sup>1,23-25</sup> BSTS and related CausalImpact-style counterfactual designs can estimate intervention-related deviations,<sup>15,26</sup> whereas Bayesian nowcasting and reporting-delay models focus on incomplete recent counts and timeliness correction.<sup>27,28</sup> These strands are therefore complementary to, rather than replacements for, a portfolio-level recovery review layer.

In digital-medicine terms, we position this study as a review-support framework for portfolio-level surveillance triage rather than as a comparison of forecasting families. We therefore develop and retrospectively evaluate a counterfactual framework for post-disruption recovery assessment in infectious disease surveillance using routine national data. Drawing on Thailand's notifiable disease surveillance system, we analysed 43 infectious diseases from 2008 to 2025 and modelled 24 diseases with stable pre-pandemic monthly time series. We examine whether an uncertainty-aware, dual-metric framework can transform retrospective surveillance data into an auditable disease-review queue by distinguishing monthly normalization from cumulative reconciliation, quantifying classification uncertainty, identifying diseases requiring cumulative follow-up or seasonal recalibration, and enabling reproducible prioritization across a national surveillance portfolio.

## Results

### Counterfactual baselines of high-burden diseases

A subset of 24 diseases with stable pre-pandemic time series accounted for 98.30% of cumulative reported cases and formed the core modelling set (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1; Supplementary Tables S2-S3). This counterfactual layer therefore covered the high-burden core of the national surveillance portfolio while leaving sparse or short-duration diseases in descriptive monitoring rather than removing them from review. The 19 diseases retained descriptively but not modelled counterfactually were concentrated in sparse, short, non-seasonal, or residual series rather than a single transmission group, indicating that model exclusion reflected time-series suitability rather than de-prioritization of specific disease classes (Supplementary Table S3). Excluding structurally sparse series also reduced the influence of low-denominator instability on forecast ranking. Disease-specific model selection identified a heterogeneous set of optimal forecasting families rather than a single dominant specification: weighted hybrid ensemble and exponential smoothing were each selected for 6 diseases, the trigonometric seasonality, Box-Cox transformation, autoregressive moving-average errors, trend, and seasonal components (TBATS) family for 5, Bayesian structural time series for 3, autoregressive integrated moving average with Fourier terms for 3, and seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average for 1 (Fig. 2A). The median within-disease separation between the best- and worst-performing candidate families was 14.30 percentage points on the symmetric mean absolute percentage error (sMAPE) scale, supporting disease-specific rather than one-size-fits-all baselines (Supplementary Figures S87-S110). Paired comparisons against uniform ETS and SARIMA baselines further favored adaptive model selection for 17 and 21 of the 24 diseases, respectively (Fig. 2B,C).

These baseline choices were stable to alternative aggregation rules, with the primary selected family recovered for most diseases under rank-based, sMAPE-only, and horizon-weighted selection variants (Supplementary Table S11). The upstream data-processing pipeline also showed high agreement with official monthly totals during overlap years from 2020 to 2023 (Pearson r = 0.998; median absolute error 3 cases; median absolute percentage error 3.90%), supporting the validity of the reconstructed monthly series used for downstream modelling (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7; Supplementary Figures S1-S86). Forecast uncertainty was then propagated from 5,000 simulated trajectories per disease into all recovery summaries.

### Framework-based surveillance priorities

Recovery was frequently decoupled between monthly normalization and cumulative reconciliation. Using the deterministic counterfactual median as the primary summary, the Return to Pre-pandemic baseline (RP) and Balance of Pre-pandemic cumulative deficit (BP) algorithm assigned the 24 modelled diseases to four surveillance-defined phenotypes (Fig. 3): 14 achieved both RP and BP within follow-up, 8 achieved RP without BP, 1 remained suppressed without RP, and malaria showed no sustained cumulative deficit. RP and BP were operational milestones rather than biological endpoints, but they identified substantively different surveillance states. Monthly normalization often arrived well before cumulative closure: hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) reached RP 9 months after January 2020 but did not achieve BP until month 33 (Fig. 3M), and chickenpox (Fig. 3C), influenza (Fig. 3A), mumps (Fig. 3D), dengue fever (Fig. 3G), hepatitis A virus (HAV, Fig. 3Q), shigellosis (Fig. 3O), scarlet fever (Fig. 3E), and chancroid  (Fig. 3W) all met RP criteria while remaining cumulatively unresolved at the end of follow-up.

Incidence-only surveillance would have substantially misclassified post-disruption recovery. By the end of follow-up, 22 of the 24 modelled diseases appeared operationally normalized on monthly incidence alone, but the framework retained only 8 of these 22 in the low-priority routine-review queue and reassigned the remaining 14 to cumulative review, recalibration, or manual-review queues with explicit reasons (Supplementary Table S12). This amounted to decision discordance for 58.33% of the full 24-disease portfolio and for 63.64% of diseases that would otherwise have been treated as normalized. The under-triage signal was concentrated in the 8 RP-without-BP diseases, which appeared recovered on monthly incidence but still carried unresolved cumulative deficits, and in 6 balanced diseases that still required seasonal recalibration despite achieving BP (Table 1).

This differentiation persisted when the framework was evaluated at earlier decision freeze points. Using only information available by December 2023, the framework queue captured all 17 diseases that still required continued review by December 2024, whereas the incidence-only comparator captured 6. At the June 2024 freeze point, the framework captured 13 of 15 diseases that still required continued review by December 2025, compared with 4 of 15 under incidence-only review (Supplementary Table S18; Supplementary Fig. S127). These freeze-point analyses reflect internal prospective agreement with later queue needs within the same rule system rather than external validation, but they still indicate improved prioritization of follow-up within the framework.

### Uncertainty and sensitivity analyses

The deterministic RP/BP labels were informative, but their stability varied across diseases (Supplementary Table S9). HAV and influenza showed stable RP-without-BP profiles, whereas HFMD, hepatitis B virus (HBV), genital herpes, *Streptococcus suis* (*S. suis*), scrub typhus, melioidosis, and typhoid showed consistently strong support for cumulative closure. Ambiguity was concentrated in a smaller subset, particularly syphilis, dengue fever, shigellosis, amebiasis, and scarlet fever, and 9 diseases had primary-status probability below 0.80 and were flagged as uncertainty-sensitive in the review layer. Uncertainty therefore qualified the strength of evidence for each phenotype without removing the central distinction between monthly and cumulative recovery.

The main classifications were also robust to alternative operational specifications. RP/BP assignments were unchanged when the monthly recovery threshold was varied across 90%, 95%, and 100%, when persistence requirements were varied from 2 to 4 months, and when the analytical interruption date was shifted from January 2020 to March or April 2020 (Supplementary Tables S5 and S10). Across all nine threshold-persistence configurations, the portfolio phenotype distribution remained identical at 14 balanced, 8 RP-only, 1 suppressed, and 1 no-deficit disease. An exploratory threshold-tolerance stress test extending the RP cutoff from 85% to 110% showed no phenotype drift for 16 of 18 threshold-persistence combinations and only a single disease-level reclassification under the strictest 110% rule (Supplementary Fig. S129). Alternative endpoint analyses supported the same interpretation but showed that not all endpoints were equally useful for decision support: a sustained observed-to-expected ratio of at least 1.0 reproduced the same achieved-versus-not-achieved distinction as the primary RP rule across all 23 diseases that entered a cumulative deficit, whereas predictive-interval return was less discriminating and half-deficit closure behaved as an intermediate milestone (Supplementary Table S13).

Calibration and falsification results qualify for this robustness. Across the three pre-pandemic placebo interruption windows, portfolio-mean 80% empirical coverage ranged from 0.568 to 0.665 and portfolio-mean 95% coverage from 0.726 to 0.792; when pooled by horizon bin, 80% coverage ranged from 0.591 to 0.634 and 95% coverage from 0.747 to 0.802, while mean WIS rose from 1932 in months 1-12 to 4626 in months 25-36 (Supplementary Table S19; Supplementary Fig. S128). values across placebo windows remained close to 0.5 (0.454-0.535), but the three placebo windows still generated 15-17 false alerts and 3-12 false-suppressed classifications per 24-disease window. False alerts were observed in all four transmission groups, with deterministic group-specific placebo false-alert rates ranging from 0.43 to 0.83, tempered rates from 0.14 to 0.83, and group-specific mean 95% coverage from 0.44 to 0.97. Disease-level falsification burden was also heterogeneous: only 2 diseases produced zero false alerts across all three placebo windows under the deterministic rule, whereas 8 produced false alerts in all three windows. Applying the tempered rule reduced false alerts to 10-13 per window and increased the number of zero-false-alert diseases to 6, showing that calibration diagnostics and decision tempering improved but did not remove spurious RP/BP states under short no-disruption windows.

### Robustness and contextual validation of recovery patterns

Re-running the full modelling and recovery workflow under count-log and rate-sqrt specifications produced only limited phenotype drift relative to the square-root count baseline. Under the count-log configuration, 2 diseases changed phenotype, 2 changed status, and 2 showed material timing shifts; under rate-sqrt, the corresponding counts were 3, 3, and 7 (Supplementary Tables S20-S21). Median absolute RP shifts were 0 months under both alternatives, and median absolute BP shifts were 1 month for count-log and 3 months for rate-sqrt. The largest timing differences were 16 months for RP and 5 months for BP under count-log, and 12 and 16 months, respectively, under rate-sqrt. This means changes in the probability of RP and BP were modest and negative on average (count-log: -0.119 and -0.113; rate-sqrt: -0.041 and -0.091), indicating that the alternative transform and denominator choice mostly perturbed confidence rather than the core queue assignment. Phenotype changes were concentrated in amebiasis, chancroid, influenza, shigellosis, and syphilis, whereas the largest timing-only shifts were seen for chickenpox, HFMD, HCV, leptospirosis, melioidosis, mumps, *S. suis*, and scrub typhus.

Portfolio suppression aligned with the period of greatest external disruption. During the restriction-intensive period of 2020-2021, the mean observed-to-expected portfolio ratio across the 24 modelled diseases was 0.41, rising to 0.53 in the 2022 transition period and 1.18 in the January 2023 to June 2024 period for which World Health Organization (WHO) case data were available (Supplementary Table S14). Across months with available external data, the monthly portfolio ratio was negatively correlated with national stringency and related response indicators, supporting the interpretation that the deepest suppression coincided with the most restrictive pandemic conditions (Supplementary Table S15). Peak stringency occurred in April 2020, the WHO COVID-19 case peak in August 2021, the first sustained low-restriction month in October 2022, and the first sustained portfolio-level normalization month in July 2023. These contextual signals were used only to interpret timing and to motivate interruption-date sensitivity analyses, not as predictive covariates.

An exploratory segmented cumulative-deviation comparator using the empirical trough as a knot was broadly concordant with the prespecified BP rule but not interchangeable with it. The primary and segmented approaches each classified 14 diseases as balanced, agreed within 6 months for 12 of 24 diseases, and left 10 diseases unresolved in both systems; no disease was exclusive to only one method. Only 2 diseases showed materially different BP timing, and the median absolute BP-month difference was 2.5 months (Supplementary Table S24). This suggests that the segmented comparator is useful as an internal benchmark, but the prespecified BP rule remains the clearer operational definition for the main framework.

Exploratory suppression-rebound summaries, correlation overlays, and descriptive typology plots are reported in Supplementary Fig. S130 and were retained only as descriptive context rather than as queue-defining evidence.

### Seasonality and comparator concordance

Recovery classification alone was insufficient to determine operational surveillance readiness, because several diseases remained seasonally displaced after apparent recovery (Fig. 4). At the prespecified >=2-month cutoff, 9 of the 24 modelled diseases were flagged as seasonally shifted in relation to pre-pandemic or post-2023 counterfactual reference profiles. Observed-to-expected ratios were below 1 for most diseases during 2020-2022 and increased from 2023 onward across all transmission categories (Fig. 4A-D). Using the weighted circular center-of-mass metric, the largest phase shifts were observed for mumps, HAV, hepatitis C virus (HCV), and condyloma acuminatum, with intermediate shifts for rubella, HBV, and genital herpes, whereas dengue fever, malaria, and *S. suis* infection showed little or no shift on this metric (Fig. 4H, J, U, X-Z, AB). Seasonal amplitude changed independently of phase timing: it was compressed for mumps, HAV, HCV, and condyloma acuminatum, but remained stable or increased for several vector-borne infections, including dengue fever, malaria, and scrub typhus.

Joint interpretation of RP/BP phenotype and seasonal displacement revealed four operational patterns that would have been missed by any single metric alone (Supplementary Table S12). Seasonally shifted but cumulatively balanced diseases, including melioidosis, HBV, condyloma acuminatum, genital herpes, HCV, and syphilis, required recalibration despite achieving BP. Mumps and HAV combined unresolved cumulative deficit with marked phase shift and therefore represented the clearest recalibrate-and-monitor targets. Chickenpox, influenza, scarlet fever, dengue fever, shigellosis, and chancroid instead fit a cumulative-review queue because their main residual issue was unresolved cumulative deficit rather than calendar displacement, whereas rubella remained a distinct manual-review case. These findings show that recovery classification alone is insufficient for operational surveillance and that seasonal displacement must be integrated into the same decision layer to align alerts and interventions with post-disruption timing.

Bootstrap uncertainty did not change the main operational reading. Across the 24 modelled diseases, 9 had seasonal shift flags and 11 had max-metric flags; 20 diseases showed agreement between the weighted center-of-mass and max-based seasonal metrics, and 16 were borderline in at least one bootstrap direction (Supplementary Table S22). Median bootstrap point shifts were 0 months versus both the pre-pandemic and post-2023 reference profiles, with median 95% interval widths of 3 months versus pre and 2 months versus predicted. Recomputing the late follow-up with a simpler day-allocation weekly-to-monthly reconstruction changed neither seasonal shift flags nor queue membership: 0 of 24 diseases changed queue assignment, and 0 changed seasonal shift status (Supplementary Table S23).

## Discussion

The main contribution of this study is to recast post-disruption surveillance recovery from a descriptive surveillance question into an auditable digital review-prioritization problem. Across Thailand's 24 modelled diseases, monthly normalization, cumulative deficit closure, and seasonal realignment frequently diverged, indicating that post-disruption recovery is multidimensional and operational rather than a single return-to-baseline event. In that sense, the framework contributes at three levels: conceptually, it treats recovery as a set of operational states rather than a binary endpoint; methodologically, it unifies counterfactual trajectories, cumulative deficit closure, seasonal displacement, and uncertainty into one rule system; and digitally, it renders those outputs into an auditable queue for human review. The main contribution is therefore not a new forecasting model per se, but a digital decision-support architecture that converts heterogeneous post-disruption surveillance trajectories into reviewable operational states across a national portfolio.

From an operational perspective, incidence-only normalization functioned as an under-triage heuristic rather than a sufficient basis for review prioritization. By the end of follow-up, 22 of the 24 modelled diseases appeared normalized on monthly incidence alone, but only 8 could be placed in the low-priority routine-review queue; the remaining 14 still required cumulative follow-up, seasonal recalibration, or manual review, corresponding to 58.33% discordance across the full portfolio. Operationally, this discordance reflects two distinct unresolved states that incidence-only review cannot separate: diseases reaching RP without BP were monthly realigned while retaining unresolved reported-burden deficit, whereas diseases achieving BP but retaining seasonal displacement were cumulatively reconciled but still required alert-calendar recalibration. The main error reduced by the framework is therefore false reassurance from apparent normalization alone.

Although the framework is not intended for mechanistic inference, the disease-level heterogeneity was epidemiologically interpretable at a broad transmission-context level. The longest RP-BP gaps were concentrated in childhood and close-contact infections such as HFMD, chickenpox, and mumps, for which school closure and reduced child mixing during the disruption period could plausibly have generated a larger susceptible backlog than would be evident from any single month of post-reopening incidence.<sup>6-11</sup> Under that pattern, reported monthly counts may return to the expected range before the accumulated disruption-era shortfall is fully reconciled. By contrast, several vector-borne and sexually transmitted infections showed shorter gaps or more prominent seasonal displacement, suggesting that the operational consequences of disruption differed across transmission routes. These contrasts should be interpreted as contextual explanations of surveillance trajectories rather than as causal evidence for immunity debt or any single mechanism.

Freeze-point analyses supported this reading, but should be interpreted conservatively. Using only information available by December 2023 and June 2024, the framework reproduced subsequent review-need classifications for 17 of 17 and 13 of 15 diseases, respectively, that still required continued review at the subsequent endpoint, compared with 6 and 4 under incidence-only review; overall freeze-point accuracy was 0.958 and 0.750 versus 0.542, but the framework also produced 1 and 4 false-positive review assignments. This should be interpreted as internal temporal agreement within the same rule system rather than as evidence of decision accuracy or real-world effectiveness. At most, these analyses suggest that queue-based review may structure analyst attention and recalibration effort more explicitly and in a manner more consistent with later follow-up needs; prospective deployment is still required to determine whether this translates into faster, more consistent, or more effective surveillance decisions.

We position the framework accordingly as a digital decision-support layer rather than as an autonomous alerting system. The scientific contribution lies in the auditable decision logic that maps heterogeneous disease trajectories to explicit review states; the interface is one implementation of that workflow rather than the primary claim of the study. Its intended use is to support analyst review rather than replace analyst judgement. In practice, the queue is interpretable because it is linked to disease-level trajectories, probability summaries, and seasonal flags, allowing low-confidence classifications, persistent BP deficits, or seasonal-shift signals to trigger analyst adjudication rather than automatic action. This human-in-the-loop framing is especially important where expert review capacity, rather than data availability, is the scarce operational resource, and the interface itself has not yet undergone prospective end-user evaluation.

The credibility of the framework rests on a chain of evidence rather than on any single diagnostic. Portfolio classifications were stable across threshold, persistence, interruption-date, transform, and denominator sensitivity analyses, suggesting that the main queue structure was not an artefact of one operational rule. At the same time, disease-level uncertainty was heterogeneous, and alternative endpoint analyses showed that some plausible recovery definitions aligned with later review needs better than others. The falsification results sharpen the boundary of appropriate interpretation: placebo windows showed sub-nominal empirical coverage and 15-17 false alerts per 24-disease window despite the absence of a true disruption, reduced but not eliminated under rule tempering. Deterministic RP/BP labels should therefore be interpreted as triage-for-review signals whose strength depends on accompanying uncertainty and sensitivity summaries, not as self-validating proof that an operational state truly exists.

A conservative operational reading follows from these diagnostics. The framework is safest when deterministic queue assignment, probabilistic confidence, and analyst override are treated as separate layers: the queue nominates review order, RP/BP probabilities express confidence, and manual adjudication resolves borderline cases. In practical terms, diseases with primary-status probability below 0.80, only partial core-sensitivity stability, or repeated placebo false alerts should not be automatically de-escalated to low-intensity review and should instead remain eligible for analyst override or shorter-interval re-review. Repeated deterministic placebo alerts occurred in 18 of 24 diseases and remained in 13 after tempering, reinforcing that the present framework is better suited to low-stakes triage than to automated closure or escalation. Current evidence therefore supports reproducible prioritization logic rather than externally validated operational superiority.

These results also clarify the intended and inappropriate uses of the framework. It can support prioritization of diseases for review, identify unresolved reported-burden deficits within routine surveillance data, and flag recalibration needs for seasonal alerting. It cannot estimate true infection burden recovery, separate transmission changes from concurrent shifts in care seeking, testing, and reporting, or attribute observed patterns causally to specific PHSM or healthcare-service disruptions.<sup>3,6-10</sup> It should not be used to infer immunity debt mechanistically, to judge transmission recovery directly, or to automate downstream action without disease-specific expert oversight.<sup>11</sup> Unresolved BP is therefore best interpreted as a persistent reported-burden deficit, and seasonal-shift flags as alert-calendar recalibration needs rather than mechanistic statements about biological phase resetting.

Because the queue redistributes finite analytic attention across diseases, deployment also has a governance and equity dimension. Auditability should extend beyond model output to the governance of thresholds, overrides, exclusions, and post-deployment monitoring of prioritization drift. In practical terms, this includes threshold governance for who defines and updates queue rules and how often they are revisited, override governance for when analysts may supersede queue assignments and whether reasons are recorded, exclusion governance for keeping sparse or non-modelled diseases visible through descriptive monitoring and manual escalation pathways, and monitoring governance for tracking failure modes such as over-prioritization, missed unresolved diseases, or systematic neglect of low-incidence conditions. Without these safeguards, a portfolio framework could inadvertently concentrate review capacity only on high-signal diseases.

Several limitations follow from this intended-use boundary. The forecasting layer used transformation-based general time-series families rather than explicit count-data or reporting-delay models. Although exclusion of sparse, weak-signal, and residual series improved stability for the high-burden portfolio core, it also limits generalizability to low-incidence, emerging, or structurally sparse conditions. This matters operationally because some rare but high-consequence diseases may still warrant the highest surveillance priority; they should remain on dedicated early-warning pathways rather than be judged through the present counterfactual module. Monthly reconstruction may still influence fine seasonal timing, even though the alternative reconstruction altered center-of-mass estimates for only one disease and did not change any shift flag or queue membership, and the >=2-month seasonal-shift cutoff remains a pragmatic operational flag rather than a formally optimized phase-uncertainty threshold. More fundamentally, the review states used here are operational constructs designed for surveillance workflow, not naturally observed ground-truth labels. Their value therefore depends on decision usefulness, auditability, and prospective agreement with expert review, rather than on correspondence to a single external biological truth. The absence of an external gold standard likewise means that freeze-point analyses evaluate temporal rule consistency rather than decision correctness. These considerations support reproducible review-support prototyping and deployment planning more directly than claims of real-world effectiveness.

A further limitation is potential model drift. The framework assumes that the 2008-2019 baseline remains a meaningful reference trajectory against which post-2020 surveillance can be judged, but some diseases may have undergone durable regime change rather than temporary displacement. Respiratory viruses such as RSV or influenza are plausible examples of infections for which seasonality and age mixing may not fully revert to the pre-pandemic regime, and similar drift could also arise through altered contact structure, changed vaccine uptake, or revised healthcare-seeking and testing behaviour. In that setting, continued comparison with the pre-pandemic baseline can eventually misclassify adaptation as non-recovery. For future deployment, the pre-pandemic counterfactual should therefore be retired once a sufficiently long and internally coherent post-disruption period has accumulated, case definitions and reporting practice are stable, and deviations from the historical baseline remain directionally persistent despite recalibration. A practical transition would be to retain the 2008-2019 baseline for historical audit of disruption recovery, but to re-estimate an operational "new normal" baseline from post-2023 data after approximately 24-36 stable months if the evidence indicates sustained regime shift.

This scope also explains why we did not frame the study as a head-to-head comparison with all surveillance algorithms. Classical aberration detectors, BSTS/CausalImpact-style approaches, and reporting-delay models have different native targets,<sup>15,23-28</sup> so they are better viewed as complementary tools than as interchangeable comparators for the review queue evaluated here.

The framework is likely most transferable to surveillance settings with multi-year monthly series, stable case definitions, documented reporting continuity, and local institutional capacity to act on queue outputs, but it should not be treated as a universal template. Local deployment would still require recalibration of thresholds, queue rules, and interpretive context, and the supplementary pertussis case study, in which 4 of 6 external series reached both RP and BP whereas 2 reached RP without BP, should be read as evidence of workflow portability rather than external validation of the Thailand rule set. Because models are fit offline and the dashboard serves cached outputs, the framework is most naturally implemented as a scheduled batch review process aligned with monthly or quarterly surveillance refreshes. Prospective evaluation should therefore examine not only predictive agreement, but also workflow, decision, safety, and usability endpoints, including time-to-review, inter-analyst consistency, override frequency, recalibration decisions, missed unresolved diseases, false-priority escalation, analyst burden, and user acceptability. The supplementary author-side heuristic task assessment supports basic interface feasibility, but it is not a substitute for prospective end-user testing.

## Conclusion

Post-disruption surveillance recovery should be treated not as a single return-to-baseline event, but as a multidimensional and auditable review problem. In Thailand's national portfolio, queue-based integration of monthly normalization, cumulative reconciliation, seasonal recalibration, and uncertainty flags reduced false reassurance from incidence-only review and clarified which disease states still warranted follow-up, recalibration, or adjudication. The framework is therefore best understood as an auditable, human-in-the-loop decision-support layer whose operational value now requires prospective evaluation, local governance, and safeguards for low-signal diseases before routine deployment.

## Methods

### Study design, data sources, and disease selection

We conducted a retrospective development and evaluation study of a digital public-health review-support framework using publicly available aggregated surveillance data for nationally notifiable infectious diseases in Thailand. The observational-data component was reported with reference to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidance.<sup>12</sup> Monthly national case counts for 2008-2024 were assembled from the Bureau of Epidemiology surveillance portal (https://doe1.moph.go.th/surdata/index.php), and weekly surveillance data for 2020-2025 were obtained from the Department of Disease Control dashboard (https://dvis3.ddc.moph.go.th/). The source registry comprised 72 monitored disease series. Annual population denominators were taken from the United Nations World Population Prospects 2024 mid-year estimates.<sup>13</sup> Surveillance data were aggregated by disease and time; sex assigned at birth, gender identity, race, and ethnicity were not consistently available and were therefore not analysed.

Disease screening followed a two-stage protocol recorded in the analytical metadata and summarized in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S0. Stage 1 retained diseases for descriptive portfolio analysis after excluding overlapping categories, non-infectious conditions, ill-defined or residual categories, zero-incidence series, incompletely reported recent series, and a series with a surveillance-definition shift (Supplementary Table S2). The 29 diseases excluded before descriptive analysis comprised 11 overlapping categories, 6 conditions outside the transmissible-disease focus, 5 ill-defined or residual series, 3 zero-incidence series, 3 incompletely reported recent series, and 1 series with a surveillance-definition shift. Stage 2 retained diseases for counterfactual modelling when they met four prespecified operational criteria: a continuous 144-month pre-pandemic monthly series from January 2008 to December 2019, a stable surveillance definition, sufficient pre-pandemic signal to support 72-month extrapolation, and absence of dominance by structural zeros. Operationally, sufficient signal referred to recurring pre-pandemic seasonal structure and month-to-month variation strong enough to support discrimination among candidate models in the rolling hold-out comparisons, whereas structural-zero dominance referred to series with so many zero months that transformation-based extrapolation would mainly track absence rather than interpretable seasonal burden. Nineteen diseases remained in descriptive monitoring only, primarily because pre-pandemic counts were too sparse for stable long-horizon forecasting (12 diseases), because the historical signal was weakly seasonal or non-seasonal (3 diseases), because duration was insufficient (2 diseases), or because residual definitions limited interpretability (2 diseases). Diseases that failed the forecasting screen therefore remained visible in the portfolio rather than being discarded, and disease-level reason labels were preserved in Supplementary Table S3 rather than being collapsed into a single exclusion category. This screening also defines the intended-use boundary of the framework. The counterfactual RP/BP module is designed for medium- to high-frequency diseases with stable multi-year monthly signal; it is not an early-warning system for newly emerging, rare, or structurally sparse diseases. Operationally, we therefore envision a dual-track surveillance portfolio in which signal-rich diseases enter counterfactual recovery review, whereas low-frequency or newly emerging conditions remain on complementary early-warning tracks such as scan-statistic surveillance (for example, SaTScan-style monitoring), Poisson/exceedance threshold alerts, descriptive review, and manual escalation.<sup>29</sup> To extend incomplete official monthly follow-up through 2025, weekly surveillance totals were temporally disaggregated to monthly counts and validated against overlap-period official monthly series; the full reconstruction protocol and validation results are reported in the Supplementary Methods, Supplementary Tables S6-S7, and Supplementary Figures S1-S86.

### Counterfactual model selection and forecasting

For each modelled disease, counterfactual trajectories from January 2020 to December 2025 were estimated from the pre-pandemic baseline spanning January 2008 to December 2019. January 2020 was chosen a priori as the common portfolio interruption date to maximize cross-disease comparability; March 2020 and April 2020 were prespecified alternative interruption dates for sensitivity analyses. Counts were modelled on the square-root scale after adding 0.01, a negligible constant used only to retain zero-count months under transformation. This common positive-support transformation allowed diverse forecasting families to be compared on the same scale; diseases dominated by structural zeros or weak signal were excluded upstream rather than forced into counterfactual extrapolation. Model fitting was performed on transformed monthly counts, whereas forecasts, simulated trajectories, thresholds, and recovery rules were interpreted on the original count scale after back-transformation.

We compared seven candidate model families spanning autoregressive neural networks, information-criterion-selected exponential-smoothing state-space models, information-criterion-selected seasonal autoregressive integrated moving-average models, trigonometric state-space models for complex seasonality with a fixed 12-month cycle, a hybrid ensemble combining autoregressive integrated moving-average, exponential-smoothing, neural-network, and TBATS components with cross-validated root mean squared error weighting, Bayesian structural time-series models with local linear trend and seasonal states, and autoregressive models augmented with Fourier harmonic terms. For the harmonic-regression specification, the number of Fourier terms was selected by minimum corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc) over candidate values of $K$. Three rolling pre-pandemic hold-out schemes were used for model selection: training through December 2018 with validation on 2019, training through December 2017 with validation on 2018-2019, and training through December 2016 with validation on 2017-2019. Forecast accuracy was assessed on the back-transformed scale using symmetric mean absolute percentage error (sMAPE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute scaled error (MASE).<sup>16</sup>

For each disease and hold-out split, each accuracy metric was z-standardized across the seven candidate models, multiplied by $-1$ so that larger values indicated better performance, and summed with equal weight to define a split-specific composite score. Composite scores were then summed across the three hold-out schemes, and the highest-scoring model was selected as the primary disease-specific counterfactual specification. This aggregation rule was specified to balance scale-dependent and scale-free error measures within each disease rather than to privilege any single metric or horizon. For the hybrid model specifically, the ensemble used the `forecastHybrid` `aent` specification with seasonal autoregressive structure enabled and `cv.errors` weights based on RMSE. To limit overfitting in shorter pre-pandemic series, the hybrid cross-validation window was constrained to be at least 24 months while preserving at least two annual cycles outside the weighting window. Alternative aggregation rules were assessed separately in robustness analyses.

After model selection, the winning specification for each disease was refit to the full January 2008-December 2019 baseline and forecast forward to December 2025. We treated this counterfactual as a pragmatic reference trajectory for portfolio triage rather than as a long-horizon causal estimand or a guarantee of forecast optimality across the full 72-month extrapolation window. Forecast uncertainty in the primary analysis was summarized from 5,000 simulated trajectories per disease, with 1,000 iterations for the Bayesian structural models. Residual-bootstrap forward simulation was used for the autoregressive neural-network, seasonal autoregressive integrated moving-average, trigonometric state-space, and Fourier-augmented autoregressive families; transformed-scale one-step residual bootstraps around the deterministic forecast were used for the exponential-smoothing family; historical-residual resampling around the mean forecast was used for the hybrid ensemble; and posterior predictive draws after burn-in were used for the Bayesian structural family. For the hybrid ensemble, uncertainty was therefore propagated by resampling historical residuals around the mean ensemble forecast rather than by refitting component models within each forward draw. Monthly medians and 80% and 95% predictive intervals were obtained from the empirical quantiles of these simulated trajectories. Empirical interval calibration was then assessed in pre-pandemic placebo interruption windows by comparing observed counts with the nominal 80% and 95% predictive intervals over 12-, 24-, and 36-month placebo follow-up periods, and by summarizing horizon-binned coverage, weighted interval score, and probability integral transform values alongside an exploratory tempered false-alert rule. This tempered rule retained a non-`No deficit` placebo alert only when at least 2 of 3 months within the persistence window also fell below the lower 80% predictive limit.

### RP/BP definitions, uncertainty propagation, and sensitivity analyses

We compared observed monthly counts after January 2020 with disease-specific counterfactual medians. For month $t$, observed cases were denoted by $O_t$ and the counterfactual median by $E_t$. Monthly deviation and cumulative deviation were defined as $D_t = O_t - E_t$ and $C_t = \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} D_{\tau}$, respectively. Disruption onset was defined as the first month for which $C_t < 0$, and the trough was the month at which $C_t$ reached its minimum.

Recovery phenotypes were mapped using Return to Pre-pandemic baseline (RP) and Balance of Pre-pandemic cumulative deficit (BP). RP was defined as the first month $t$ for which observed monthly counts satisfied $O_t \ge 0.95E_t$ for three consecutive months and the cumulative deviation was non-decreasing month-to-month over the same window. This additional cumulative condition was included to avoid declaring recovery in windows where monthly incidence temporarily met threshold while the cumulative deficit was still worsening. BP was defined as the first month after the trough at which $C_t \ge 0$. We treated the 95% threshold and 3-month persistence rule as prespecified operational rules for monthly surveillance review rather than as biological endpoints. Their rationale was asymmetric and portfolio-governance oriented: the cost of prematurely downgrading a disease that still warranted follow-up was judged higher than the cost of temporarily over-reviewing a disease that later proved resolved. Operationally, 95% was used as a tolerance band around the expected monthly burden: modest shortfalls can remain compatible with practical realignment in the presence of reporting noise, whereas much looser cutoffs would risk masking sizeable unresolved deficits and exact equality can delay recognition without changing review need. The common thresholds should therefore be read as cross-disease governance heuristics designed to reduce false reassurance, not as disease-mechanistic or decision-theoretically optimal cut-points for every pathogen. These defaults were not data-optimized against the retrospective freeze-point outcomes in order to avoid overfitting the queue rules to the same portfolio used for evaluation; instead, they were retained for interpretability and then stress-tested through threshold-persistence, interruption-date, falsification, and transform/rate sensitivity analyses. To make this epidemiological tolerance explicit, we report the prespecified 90-100% threshold range in Supplementary Table S5 and an exploratory threshold-tolerance stress test spanning 85-110% with persistence requirements of 2-4 months in Supplementary Fig. S129.

To propagate uncertainty into recovery classification, we applied the RP/BP algorithm to each of the 5,000 simulated counterfactual trajectories, yielding disease-specific probabilities of achieving RP and BP, empirical 95% intervals for RP/BP timing, and the probability of retaining the deterministic median-based phenotype. Sensitivity analyses repeated the classification under alternative interruption dates (January, March, and April 2020), alternative RP thresholds (90%, 95%, and 100%), and alternative persistence requirements (2-4 months). Additional endpoint analyses examined predictive-interval return, sustained observed-to-expected ratio of at least 1.0, and half-deficit closure. To assess dependence on scale and denominator choice, we repeated the full recovery workflow under count-log and rate-sqrt specifications and compared phenotype concordance, timing shifts, and probability changes against the primary square-root count baseline. Suppression and rebound descriptors used for supplementary typology analyses (Supplementary Fig. S130) were not part of the primary RP/BP decision rule.

### Seasonal displacement and retrospective decision-point assessment

Seasonal shape, amplitude, and timing were compared across three periods: pre-pandemic observed data through 2019, observed data from 2023 onward after relaxation of the most restrictive public-health and social measures (PHSM) phase, and the corresponding counterfactual predictions from 2023 onward. The 2022 calendar year was treated as a transition period and excluded from seasonal-shift estimation to reduce mixed-regime contamination. Seasonal timing was summarized with a weighted circular center-of-mass metric in which calendar months were mapped onto a 12-month circle and weighted by mean monthly counts; resulting shifts were corrected to the minimal signed displacement on the annual cycle.

For operational classification, substantial seasonal displacement was flagged a priori when the absolute center-of-mass shift relative to either the pre-pandemic observed profile or the post-2023 counterfactual profile was at least 2 months. This threshold was used as a pragmatic monthly-resolution rule: shifts smaller than about one surveillance month in either direction were unlikely to alter review timing, whereas shifts of 2 months or more could plausibly move peak-oriented preparedness windows to a different part of the annual review calendar. We therefore treated the cutoff as a high-signal recalibration flag rather than as a formal estimator of seasonal phase uncertainty or a disease-specific biological threshold. The seasonal module was applied only to the screened modelling subset, and shift flags were interpreted jointly with amplitude profiles and RP/BP phenotype rather than as standalone evidence. We did not elevate amplitude change to a separate queue-defining rule because the primary operational question was whether the timing of preparedness and alert review required recalibration; amplitude remained contextual information for analyst interpretation rather than a distinct automatic routing criterion. Bootstrap resampling of year-level monthly profiles quantified uncertainty around the center-of-mass shift estimates, and a simpler day-allocation weekly-to-monthly reconstruction was used as an alternative sensitivity analysis for the late follow-up period. Under that alternative reconstruction, center-of-mass point estimates changed by only 1 month for 1 disease and no shift flag or queue changed. Because neither the bootstrap uncertainty analysis nor the alternative reconstruction changed queue membership, we retained the 2-month cutoff as a conservative operational flag rather than retrofitting it to maximize retrospective queue capture. We then compared an incidence-only queue, in which diseases were considered operationally normalized once RP was achieved, against a framework-based queue that combined RP/BP phenotype with seasonal displacement into six review categories: low-priority routine review, cumulative review needed, recalibrate and monitor, balanced but recalibrate seasonality, high-priority manual review, and no-deficit monitoring. Operationally, this rule set can be read as a compact decision tree: deficit onset marks entry into cumulative tracking; the trough marks maximal cumulative shortfall; RP marks sustained monthly realignment without further deficit worsening; BP marks cumulative closure after the trough; persistent suppression triggered high-priority manual review; RP without BP triggered cumulative review unless accompanied by substantial seasonal shift, in which case the disease entered recalibrate-and-monitor; BP with substantial seasonal shift triggered recalibration; BP without shift triggered low-priority routine review; and no sustained deficit remained in no-deficit monitoring.

To assess whether this queue structure retained value at realistic decision points, we performed retrospective freeze-point emulation at December 2023 and June 2024 using only information available up to each freeze date and then re-evaluated diseases at the end of the subsequent follow-up window under the same rule set. System-level falsification analyses repeated the workflow using placebo interruption windows beginning in January 2017, January 2018, and January 2019. Any placebo classification other than `No deficit` was counted as a false alert, and placebo classifications of `Suppressed` were also tallied separately as false-suppressed. We also fit an exploratory segmented cumulative-deviation comparator that placed a single knot at the empirical trough and extrapolated the post-trough slope to estimate a complementary BP month. For descriptive contextual triangulation, national Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker indicators and WHO COVID-19 case counts were aggregated to calendar months through December 2022 and used only to contextualize disruption timing rather than as predictive covariates.

### Supplementary implementation details and exploratory analyses

Interactive Shiny applications were developed to present precomputed portfolio queues, counterfactual trajectories, uncertainty summaries, and seasonal outputs for deployment-oriented review. These interfaces load bundled cached outputs rather than refitting models at runtime, and the upstream analytical scripts use multicore execution where available to support portfolio-scale reruns. The interfaces were implementation aids rather than part of the primary inferential protocol, and formal end-to-end runtime benchmarking was not part of the present evaluation. A supplementary author-side heuristic coverage check examined six predefined review tasks using a 4-point rubric for discoverability, interpretability, and auditability; all six tasks were directly supported, with a mean score of 3.72 out of 4 and a median minimum interaction count of 1.

Additional exploratory analyses were reported only in the supplementary materials and did not define the main framework outputs. These included seasonal-trend decomposition using Loess (STL), segmented log-linear regression, suppression-rebound summaries and descriptive typology plots (Supplementary Fig. S130), and k-means clustering of standardized suppression and rebound features used only for descriptive visual grouping rather than queue construction, as well as a supplementary transportability exercise applying the same model-selection and RP/BP workflow to six external pertussis surveillance series from Australia, China, Japan, New Zealand, Sweden, and the United States.<sup>20,21,22</sup> In that cross-setting demonstration, 4 of the 6 external series reached both normalization and balance, whereas 2 reached RP without BP by the end of follow-up, supporting workflow portability across countries and cadences without constituting external validation of the Thailand rule set. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.2.
